Signal 1

Similar scores

For every school with 2 or more students, this measures how different the students' scores are from one another. On a real exam, students differ in ability, so scores vary. If everyone at a school scored almost the same, that is unusual and the school is treated as higher risk.

Signal 2

Same wrong answers

For every pair of students at a school, this compares their answers question by question. When an answer key is available, only matches where both students chose the same wrong answer count. Two students choosing the same wrong option again and again is very unlikely by chance, while matching on the correct answer proves nothing.

Combination

0-100 cheating rate

Each school is compared with every other school on both signals, and the results are combined into one score from 0 to 100. A higher score means a higher risk. When an answer key is available, the same-wrong-answers signal counts for more.

Evidence test

Question-by-question proof

For each question, the dashboard finds the wrong answer that a school's students chose most unusually often and asks: if these students had answered independently, how likely is it that this many of them picked that same option, given how often students with a similar overall score pick it? The odds are worked out exactly and adjusted for the number of questions tested. When the odds of luck are worse than about 1 in 1,000, students answering independently is not a believable explanation. The evidence panel below shows this for any high-risk school, along with its real answer sheet.

Caveat

This is a guide, not proof

It points out patterns that are consistent with students cheating together. It cannot tell cheating apart from a group that is genuinely similar and well taught. Use it to decide which schools to review first, not as a verdict. A stricter statistical version of the same idea (an IRT-based test with false-discovery control) is available as a companion Python script.

Model check-up

This section trains two standard machine-learning models to recognise the pattern of a cheating school and score every school for how well it matches that pattern. Each school is described by four numbers: its score spread, its same-wrong-answers rate, its average score, and its number of students.

  • Random Forest — hundreds of decision trees, each grown on a random sample of schools and a random subset of the four numbers, then averaged together. No single tree can dominate the answer, which makes the result stable.
  • Gradient Boosted Trees — the technique behind tools such as XGBoost. Trees are grown one after another, and each new tree focuses on the schools the previous trees got wrong. Often the most accurate model for a table of numbers like this one.

A single Decision Tree is not used on its own here: one tree memorises noise easily, which is exactly why both models above combine many trees instead of relying on one. A generic "ensemble" is what Random Forest and Gradient Boosted Trees already are.

Only schools with at least 10 students are used. With fewer students, a school's numbers swing wildly from ordinary chance alone. Schools below 10 students are covered by the rule-based check and evidence in Methodology instead.

What matters most to the models

Random Forest tracks, across every tree and every split, how much each number actually helped separate the two kinds of school. Higher bars mean that number carried more of the decision.

What this does and does not prove

A model score is not a confession. A school can score high for honest reasons: a very small or very large class, or a paper that suited its students unusually well. And the cross-validated accuracy above measures how well the models reproduce their training labels on schools they did not train on — it is not proof that the labels themselves are correct. Use a "both models agree" result the way you would a "High risk" result elsewhere in this dashboard: as a strong reason to look closer, not as a finished conclusion.

Handling high-risk schools fairly

  • Treat "High risk" as under review, not guilty. Share the evidence with the school and give it a chance to respond before any decision.
  • Remember that a wrong answer taught in class can make many honest students choose the same option. Ask teachers what was covered before concluding anything.
  • Hold certificates and prizes for high-risk schools until the review closes, and consider a short supervised re-test for a sample of their students.
  • Cross-check attendance registers, seating plans and invigilator reports for the same schools.
  • Document each step and decision so the process can be audited.

Before the next round

  • Use several versions of the paper with shuffled question order and answer options. Copying from a neighbour then stops working, and any remaining match becomes much stronger evidence.
  • Strengthen invigilation: train invigilators, use outside invigilators at centres with a history of high-risk results, and keep a written seating plan.
  • Secure the sitting: control how papers are distributed and collected, run every centre in one time window, and record submission times so groups submitting together can be spotted.
  • Announce the checks in advance. Schools that know answer patterns are analysed are less likely to risk it.

Analysis and data quality

  • Confirm before acting: run the stricter IRT/GBT test (the companion Python script), which produces formal p-values with false-discovery control.
  • Track schools across rounds. A school marked high risk in several independent rounds is far stronger evidence than one round on its own.
  • Clean school names and codes. The same school can appear under several spellings, which splits its students into separate records and can hide or create patterns.
  • Schools with only one student cannot be scored.

Recognise strong, honest performance

  • Publicly recognise the top clean schools (see Data analysis) and consider inviting their best students to the next training programme.
  • Share what these schools do well, such as teacher training and practice routines, with schools that struggle.
